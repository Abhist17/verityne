-------------------------------------------------------------------------
0:00 - 0:35   THE PROBLEM                                      (~85 words)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

[On camera, or on the landing page.]

  Every KYC vendor will show you a ROC curve and a number that sounds good.

  The problem is that anyone onboarding a fake merchant today has Stable
  Diffusion and DeepFaceLab on their laptop. They can generate a face that
  has never existed, put it on a document that looks real, and record a
  liveness video of a person who isn't there.

  So the question isn't "what's your AUC". It's "where does your system
  fail, and do you know?"

  This is Verityne. Six detectors, a fusion layer, and a published record of
  everything we got wrong building it.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
0:35 - 1:35   ARCHITECTURE                                    (~150 words)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

[Switch to the architecture diagram. One minute, don't rush it.]

  A packet comes in: a selfie, an ID document, a liveness clip, and the
  timing of how the form was filled.

  Five detectors run concurrently. Selfie deepfake - a pretrained transformer
  plus a frequency head we fitted. ID forensics - OCR, check digits,
  compression analysis. Liveness - identity drift across video frames.
  Metadata - EXIF and generator tags. And behavioral - keystroke rhythm.

  Face match runs second, not because it's less important, but because it
  needs the face crops the first stage already decoded.

  Then two things happen that matter.

  First, linkage. We hash every asset and embed every face, and check them
  against every prior submission. That's how you catch a ring.

  Second, fusion. A calibrated logistic regression turns six scores into one,
  and per-merchant policy turns that into PASS, REVIEW or REJECT.

  Here's the part I'd point at: linkage can only ever RAISE risk, never lower
  it. And a face match can't reject anyone on its own - it's capped, and sent
  to a human instead. I'll explain why later.

2:25 - 3:10   RINGS: THREAT AND REVIEW                        (~110 words)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

[/threat]

  One fake merchant is an incident. Forty sharing a face is organised crime,
  and you only see it if you link them.

  These are fraud rings - submissions connected by a shared face or a shared
  file. Five clusters here, four with byte-identical file reuse.

[/review]

  Same data, a reviewer's view. Twenty-seven cases waiting on a human.

  Look at these three - Farah Sheikh, Nazia Rahman, Sadia Iqbal. Different
  names. Same photograph. Eleven of the twenty-seven carry that badge.

  Now, why can't a face match reject on its own? Because it's a similarity
  search across every record you hold, and its false-accept rate compounds
  with database size. We measured it: at the threshold we originally shipped,
  a genuine applicant false-linked to a stranger ninety-seven percent of the
  time against a five thousand record scan. A signal with that error profile
  gets a human, not an automatic rejection.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
3:10 - 4:05   THE NUMBERS                                     (~135 words)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

[/metrics]

  Here's where most demos get vague. We won't.

  Held-out ROC-AUC: 0.753. On an identity-disjoint split - no face that
  trained the fusion layer appears in these numbers.

  That is not a great score, and I want to be precise about why it's the
  honest one. It used to be 0.913. Then an ablation showed that our own
  corpus generator had written the label into the EXIF metadata. Seventeen
  percent of the corpus was carrying a fraud label in disguise. We fixed the
  corpus, and the number went down by sixteen points.

  This chart is the fused curve against each detector alone. The fusion
  should dominate. Where a single detector beats it, our weights are wrong -
  so we show it rather than crop it.

  And the confusion matrix at the reject threshold: we catch thirty-seven
  percent of fraud automatically, at a two percent false reject rate.
  Seventy-six percent of fraud reaches a human. We chose that operating point
  from a cost curve, not by taste.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
4:05 - 4:50   WHAT WE GOT WRONG                               (~115 words)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

[/corrections]

  This is the page I actually care about.

  Nine things this project believed, measured, and turned out to be wrong
  about. Four fixed. Three still open. Every one linked to the evidence file
  that disproved it.

  Two examples.

  Our selfie detector scored 0.997 on diffusion fakes - the one attack class
  we thought we'd solved. Then we tested it on four generator families from
  datasets we did not create. It's at or below chance on all four. The worst
  is 0.123, which is below fifty percent, which means inverted - it scores
  those fakes as MORE genuine than real faces.

  And our keystroke detector hits a perfect 1.000 against every automation
  strategy we had. So we built the attack that beats it: replay a real
  person's recorded typing rhythm. It drops to 0.500. Chance.

  We published both.

=========================================================================
LIKELY QUESTIONS
=========================================================================

Q: "0.753 is low. Why should we take this seriously?"
   Because the alternative on offer is 0.913 that isn't real. We found the
   leak in our own corpus and reported the drop. Ask any vendor showing you
   0.95 how they built their negative set.

Q: "Isn't the corpus synthetic?"
   The packet corpus is, and it's small - 105 held-out. That's why the
   detectors are also measured on data we did not generate: LFW for identity,
   MIDV-2020 for documents, third-party deepfake datasets for the selfie
   detector, and 168,595 real people for keystrokes. The third-party numbers
   are the bad ones, and they're on the page.

Q: "Why is it slow / why can't I upload?"
   The hosted link runs without the models so it fits a free tier - it reads
   verdicts rather than computing them, and labels them as stored. The GPU
   link scores live in about 2.5 seconds.

Q: "What would you do next?"
   The three open findings, in order: decouple demography from the label in
   the corpus and refit the frequency head; get the selfie detector to
   generalise to unseen generators; and treat a replayed keystroke recording
   as a reuse problem rather than a timing problem - which is the linkage
   machinery we already have.
